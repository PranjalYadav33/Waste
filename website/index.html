Why household waste segregation fails between the kitchen and the collection point — evidence from three housing typologies in Mumbai.

Recommendations R1–R9 Full report Dataset · N=100

Pranjal Yadav (202503021, S.Y.B.Sc. Computer Science) · Guide: Asst. Prof. Ashish Yadav
Faculty of Science & Technology · Academic Year 2025–26

We predict that fixing urban waste is not a collection problem but a segregation-at-handover problem — and that the required fix differs by housing type.[1]

India collects 91.8% of municipal solid waste yet treats only 53.7% (CPCB 2021–22: 1,70,339 tonnes per day generated; 37,373 tonnes per day unaccounted).[3] Official portals claim 90.1% of wards fully segregate; independent field audits verify only 48.8%.[6][7] Our survey of 100 residents explains why: 53% use a single mixed bin, 67% are served by single-chamber vehicles, and 87% have watched collection staff remix separated bags.

What is this? How was it written? Why is it valuable?

What is this? A B.Sc. field project combining official records (CPCB, SBM-Urban with Garbage Free City verification, MPCB) with a digital survey of 100 residents across gated societies, chawl/market streets and community-bin pockets.

How was it written? Secondary data first — every figure in the Findings chapter regenerates from retained extracts — then primary evidence: a ten-item questionnaire, duplicate-checked, coded and cross-tabulated, with all charts on this site computed live from the published dataset.

Why is it valuable? Mission dashboards circulate claimed and verified figures without reconciling them. This study places the two side by side, traces failure to the exact handover step (domestic worker, vehicle, timing), and sets out nine recommendations with responsible agencies and measurable indicators.

Principal findings

1. Collection is largely resolved; segregation at handover is not. Of every 100 tonnes generated nationally, approximately 92 are collected but only 54 are treated; roughly 22 fall into an unaccounted gap and 24 are landfilled.[3]

2. Reported and verified segregation diverge substantially. Reported ward-level segregation rises from 60% to 90.1% across the mission period, while field verification records 48.8% — a 41.3-point difference this report treats as the central measurement problem.[6][7]

3. Treatment performance is not explained by waste volume. The correlation between generation volume and treated share across major states is r = 0.131. Administrative and infrastructural factors, not city size, account for the observed variation.[3]

4. Household behaviour responds to infrastructure, then decays without follow-up. A Delhi randomised trial recorded segregation rising from 3.69% to 54.0% within one week of bin provision with instructions and a small incentive, declining to 43% by week five.[11] High willingness without matching behaviour is documented in Mumbai (86.36% aware, 90.91% willing),[12] and an 11-point sorting gain from labelling and lids alone is documented at IIT-Mandi.[14]
