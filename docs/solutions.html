CHAPTER 3 · SECTIONS 3.5–3.6

Conclusions and recommendations

3.5 Conclusions

First, collection coverage is substantially resolved while segregation at the point of handover is not. With 91.8% of waste collected and 53.7% treated, the binding constraint is the quality of segregation where households transfer waste to the collection system, rather than the extent of vehicle coverage.

Second, the mode of failure differs by housing typology, a result consistent across field observation and survey cross-tabulation. Gated societies exhibit delegation failure at the domestic-worker and housekeeping transfer step; dense chawl and market streets exhibit a space constraint, with approximately 100 sq ft tenements unable to accommodate three bins; informal settlements exhibit timing-and-distance failure, in which a single missed hopper round produces corner dumping.

Third, the barriers admit a stable ranking: (i) absence of compartmentalised transport and proximate dual bins; (ii) irregular collection timing with delays of several hours; (iii) illegible or absent signage, excluding handlers; (iv) an unsegregated sanitary and hazardous fraction; and (v) decay of behavioural gains in the absence of scheduled follow-up.

Fourth, household willingness is considerable and currently underutilised. The Mulund survey records 86.36% awareness with 90.91% stated willingness, and the Delhi trial demonstrates a 50.3-point improvement within one week when bins, instructions and a small incentive are provided together — followed by an 11-point decline by week five. The implication is that infrastructure provision should precede further awareness expenditure in high-awareness wards, and that re-audit at approximately five weeks is a necessary component of any intervention.

Fifth, the binding constraint is institutional rather than informational. The Solid Waste Management Rules, 2016 have mandated three-stream segregation, user fees, spot fines and the integration of waste pickers for eight years, yet the Comptroller and Auditor General's 2024 audit of 102 urban local bodies found only 3.13% of collected waste was segregated, and in 11 of 13 test-checked bodies waste pickers were neither recognised nor registered.[15][19] The party missing from most implementation plans is the informal recovery sector, which this study observed within 100 metres of the Zone C collection point but did not treat as a participant in the system.

Sixth, tonnage arithmetic reframes the problem. Wet waste exceeds half of urban municipal solid waste while dry recyclables are only 5–15% by mass,[17][20] so segregation alone cannot reduce the dominant flow. Measured interventions must combine segregation at source with diversion of wet waste at or near the point of generation, and must be designed around the recovery sector rather than around household persuasion.

Recommendations

Report verified segregation alongside portal claims

Require every urban local body plan to present segregation performance in two adjacent columns: self-reported MIS figures and independently verified figures of the Garbage Free City or field-audit type. The 41.3-point discrepancy documented in this study should be treated as a standing measurement item rather than a one-time observation.

Rationale
Self-certified portal data and field verification currently circulate without reconciliation, preventing credible target-setting.
Implementing agency
Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs; urban local bodies.
Monitoring indicator
Revised dashboard template in use within one quarter.
Evidence
Figure F-2.

Direct processing investment to the six low-processing states

Prioritise material recovery facility and composting capacity in West Bengal, Andhra Pradesh, Rajasthan, Punjab, Jharkhand and Assam, which treat between 22% and 42% of generated waste despite substantial volumes. Generation volume is not predictive of treatment share and should not govern allocation.

Rationale
Management and infrastructure shortfalls, not city size, account for interstate variation.
Implementing agency
State sanitation missions.
Monitoring indicator
Number of commissioned plants by state and year.
Evidence
Figure F-3; Table T-3.1 of the report.

Introduce four-stream collection with a separate sanitary and hazardous round

Collect wet, dry, sanitary and domestic hazardous waste as distinct streams, with a weekly dedicated round for sanitary and hazardous items (sanitary pads, diapers, glass, medicines and equivalent). Weigh the collected fraction and publish weekly quantities.

Rationale
Sanitary and hazardous waste constitutes approximately 12% of mass and effectively all handling risk; a two-stream design cannot isolate it.
Implementing agency
Urban local bodies with State Pollution Control Boards.
Monitoring indicator
Kilograms per week of separately collected sanitary/hazardous waste.
Evidence
Figure F-5; survey Q7.

Complete physical infrastructure before further awareness expenditure in high-awareness wards

In wards where awareness already exceeds approximately 80%, allocate funds first to compartmentalised collection vehicles, proximate dual bins, permanent multilingual labels and engineered lids. The IIT-Mandi record of an 11% improvement in correct sorting within three days from labelling and lids alone indicates the expected order of operations.

Rationale
High willingness without corresponding behaviour (Mulund: 86.36% aware, 90.91% willing) demonstrates that information is not the binding input where hardware is absent.
Implementing agency
Urban local body engineering departments.
Monitoring indicator
Change in correct-sorting share within two weeks of installation.
Evidence
Figure F-6; survey Q5–Q6.

Combine a differentiated user fee with arrival notification and scheduled re-audit

Apply a reduced user fee to segregating households; operate an SMS or WhatsApp notification approximately 15 minutes before the vehicle's arrival (requested by 60% of respondents); and repeat measurement at five weeks to detect the decay documented in the Delhi trial. Where compartmentalised vehicles are unavailable in the short term, schedule wet waste daily in the morning and dry recyclables twice weekly so that a single-chamber vehicle does not combine them.

Rationale
Behavioural gains of approximately 50 points are attainable but decline by approximately 11 points within five weeks without reinforcement.
Implementing agency
Urban local body revenue and information-technology cells.
Monitoring indicator
Segregation rate at week one against week five, by lane.
Evidence
Figure F-6; survey Q9.

Determine bin numbers and positions analytically

Adopt the Thiruvananthapuram formulation — required bins equal (population × per-capita generation − source-eliminated quantity) ÷ bin capacity — with bin positions determined by least-distance mapping, and publish per-ward fill data. The computation is suitable as a supervised student technical contribution in collaboration with the urban local body.

Rationale
Drum overflow with prolonged exposure in Zones B and C reflects ad hoc siting rather than demand-based provision.
Implementing agency
Urban local body with student technical support.
Monitoring indicator
Overflow-hours per ward per week.
Evidence
FOSSEE Thiruvananthapuram fellowship record; Chapter 1 observation matrix.

Apply typology-differentiated provisions

Because failure mechanisms differ by dwelling type, a uniform campaign is not indicated. Detailed provisions are tabulated below (Section 3.7).

Rationale
Delegation, space and timing failures require distinct instruments; a single design cannot address all three.
Implementing agency
Corporate social responsibility sponsors; housing-society committees; ward offices.
Monitoring indicator
Change in contamination rate (CR = misplaced weight ÷ stream weight × 100) by lane.
Evidence
Chapter 1 zones; Q1 cross-tabulation.

Maintain the dataset as a living panel

Re-administer the ten-item questionnaire quarterly, append results to Annexure D, and refresh Figures F-1–F-6 annually from CPCB and SBM releases. Single-round measurement cannot track behavioural decay or verify portal claims over time.

Rationale
Both behaviour and official series change between rounds; continuity is required for inference.
Implementing agency
Student investigator with project guide.
Monitoring indicator
Quarterly appended dataset with version control.
Evidence
Annexure D.

Conduct a supervised labelling and three-day observation exercise

Affix permanent bilingual (Marathi/Hindi) sorting labels to the residential collection bins of one building and record overflow and contamination over a three-day observation window, using the contamination-rate definition above. This constitutes the student-level field action of the present project.

Rationale
Faded or monolingual labelling was recorded on 2 of 5 bins inspected in Zone A; handler legibility is a necessary condition for correct sorting.
Implementing agency
Student investigator.
Monitoring indicator
Three-day bin contamination log.
Evidence
Chapter 1 photo log.

System-level measures

The first nine measures address the household and the collection interface. The five that follow address the two structural gaps identified in the conclusions: the absence of a formal role for the informal recovery sector, and the absence of any wet-waste diversion. Both are already mandated or demonstrated elsewhere, so neither requires new legislation.

Integrate and formalise waste pickers and kabadiwalas

Identify and register waste pickers in the ward, issue identity cards, pay for recovered material by weight at market rate, provide segregated collection vehicles including twin-bin tricycles, and convert existing dhalaos into neighbourhood recycling centres. Every documented city-scale success reviewed for this study — Vengurla, Kumbakonam, Leh and the Delhi SDMC model — combines segregation with formalised informal-sector participation. Bins and awareness alone have not produced full segregation anywhere in the published record.

Rationale
The 2024 CAG audit found pickers neither recognised nor registered in 11 of 13 test-checked urban local bodies, while SWM Rules 2016 Rule 15(c) already requires their integration. The capital cost is administrative rather than financial, because the value already exists in the material they currently sell informally at a discount.
Implementing agency
Urban local body with the SWM cell and registered self-help groups.
Monitoring indicator
Count of registered pickers, and kilograms purchased per month.
Evidence
Field observation, Zone C; references 15, 16, 18, 19.

Separate streams in time where vehicles cannot be compartmentalised

Until compartmentalised vehicles are available, schedule wet waste daily in the morning and dry recyclables on fixed alternate days, so that a single-chamber vehicle never carries both streams in one trip. This requires a change to route scheduling only, with no capital expenditure.

Rationale
67% of respondents report a single-chamber vehicle, and 87% have observed mixing. Fleet replacement is slow and expensive, but scheduling is within a ward office's control this quarter. This is the cheapest single intervention available to the study's evidence.
Implementing agency
Urban local body operations.
Monitoring indicator
Share of lanes on alternate-day dry collection.
Evidence
Survey Q5–Q6; reference 16.

Apply enforcement to the mixing actor, and make it conditional on service provision

Issue spot fines under the Solid Waste Management Rules, 2016 primarily against handlers, vendors and commercial establishments that remix segregated streams, rather than against households in lanes where dual bins and labelled vehicles have not been provided.

Rationale
The present survey found downstream mixing observed by 87% of respondents while only 19% always segregate at source, placing the majority of the failure downstream of the household. Fining generators for a service failure that has not been supplied is both unjust and ineffective.
Implementing agency
Urban local body enforcement with the ward office.
Monitoring indicator
Mixing incidents recorded at handover; contamination rate in serviced against unserviced lanes.
Evidence
Survey Q2, Q6; reference 19.

Divert wet waste at source through community composting

Establish community composting units for gated societies and mandatory wet-waste segregation for commercial establishments, and exclude composted wet waste from the daily collection route. Households and institutions above 5,000 sq ft are already required by Rule 12 to segregate, and restaurants are required to segregate biodegradable waste under Rule 15.

Rationale
Wet waste exceeds half of urban generation while dry recyclables are only 5–15% by mass. Segregation reorders the waste but does not reduce the dominant tonnage, so community treatment is the only measure that lowers collected volume.
Implementing agency
Urban local body with society management committees.
Monitoring indicator
Share of wet weight diverted from the collection route.
Evidence
References 8, 16, 17, 20.

Set targets against the segregable fraction rather than against absolutes

State municipal targets against the World Bank estimate that 15–20% of national waste is conveniently segregable at source, and against the CPCB composition range of 30–55% compostable and 5–15% recyclable, rather than against claimed rates of 90% or 100% that this study has already shown to be unverified.

Rationale
A target of 100% segregation is not physically available from any Indian household waste stream. Publishing the basis of each target makes performance auditable and prevents the reporting gap documented in F-2.
Implementing agency
MoHUA and urban local bodies.
Monitoring indicator
Published basis for each municipal target.
Evidence
Figure F-2; references 16, 17, 20.
