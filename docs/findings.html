CHAPTER 3 · SECTION 3.4 (SECONDARY DATA)

Secondary-data analysis

Sources: CPCB Annual Report 2021–22 (Table-1); SBM-Urban MIS with Garbage Free City 2023 verification; MPCB quarterly Q4-2020 and annual 2023 filings; five published field studies. Each figure below is generated from retained extracts and is reproducible.

F-1 · National flow: collection succeeds, processing does not

Of the 1,70,339 tonnes per day generated nationally in 2021–22, 1,56,449 TPD (91.8%) were collected but only 91,511 TPD (53.7%) were treated. A further 41,455 TPD reached landfill and 37,373 TPD remained unaccounted for. Fourteen states report complete collection; only five report complete segregation, indicating that the binding constraint lies downstream of collection.[3]

F-2 · Mission trend: reported segregation rises, verification does not

Reported ward-level segregation increases from 60% through 74.8% to 90.1% over the mission period, while independent field verification records 48.8% — a difference of 41.3 percentage points. Reported processing rises from 18% through 60% to approximately 75%, against a verified anchor of 53.7%. Door-to-door collection coverage (94–100%) is effectively resolved; verified segregation is not.[6][7]

F-3 · State contrast: generation volume does not explain treatment performance

The correlation between generation volume and treated share across major states is r = 0.131, which is negligible. West Bengal (13,709 TPD, 22.2% treated) and Karnataka (13,034 TPD, 41.7% treated) generate at Maharashtra's order of magnitude while treating at between one-quarter and one-half of its rate. By contrast, Gujarat (86.0%), Maharashtra (84.9%) and Telangana (77.9%) form the upper band. Investment priority follows management performance, not population.[3]

F-4 · Maharashtra: documented improvement with one reporting qualification

Maharashtra's treated share rises from 63.0% (Q4-2020, 23,607 TPD) to 81.8% (Annual 2023, 24,299 TPD), with the untreated gap declining from 6,187 to approximately 873 TPD. Installed capacity is documented (456 compost, 145 vermi-compost, 57 biomethanation, 4 waste-to-energy and 23 refuse-derived-fuel plants; 409 of 418 urban local bodies reporting). One qualification applies: the 98.66% segregation figure is urban local body portal self-certification and should be read alongside independent verification of the Garbage Free City type.[4][5]

F-5 · Waste composition: the four-stream requirement

The Thanisandra Ward 6 pilot (121 households, 286.3 kg/day) records wet waste at 71.5% (204.74 kg/day), dry waste at 16.2%, sanitary waste at 8.1% and hazardous waste at 4.2%. Sanitary and hazardous fractions together constitute approximately 12% of mass while accounting for effectively all handling risk (recorded lead 12.03, cadmium 0.44 and mercury 0.195 mg/kg). A two-stream collection design cannot isolate this fraction; a separate periodic sanitary/hazardous round is indicated.[10]

F-6 · Behavioural evidence: infrastructure produces rapid gains; gains decay without follow-up

A Delhi randomised controlled trial (bin provision with brochure and a small incentive) recorded segregation rising from 3.69% to 54.0% within one week (+50.3 points), declining to 43% by week five (−11 points). A Mulund survey records 86.36% awareness and 90.91% willingness with no corresponding behaviour in the absence of bins and compartmentalised vehicles. An IIT-Mandi intervention records an 11% improvement in correct sorting within three days from permanent labelling with engineered lids alone. The consistent implication is that physical provision precedes further awareness expenditure, and that re-audit at approximately five weeks is necessary to arrest decay.[11][12][14]
